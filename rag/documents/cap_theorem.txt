CAP Theorem

The CAP theorem states that a distributed data store can only guarantee two of three properties at the same time: consistency, availability, and partition tolerance. Consistency means every read receives the most recent write or an error; availability means every request receives a non-error response, without a guarantee it contains the most recent write; partition tolerance means the system continues operating despite network failures that prevent some nodes from communicating with others.

In practice, partition tolerance is not really an optional design choice for any distributed system running over a real network, because network partitions -- dropped packets, downed links, split data centers -- will eventually happen regardless of how reliable the infrastructure is. This means the actual choice most distributed systems face is not "which two of three," but specifically "when a partition occurs, do we prioritize consistency or availability."

Systems that prioritize consistency during a partition (often called CP systems) will refuse to serve some requests, or will return errors, rather than risk returning stale or conflicting data. Traditional relational databases configured for strong consistency, and coordination systems like ZooKeeper or etcd, generally fall into this category. Systems that prioritize availability (AP systems) continue answering requests even when nodes are cut off from each other, accepting that different nodes might temporarily disagree about the current state, with that disagreement resolved later once the partition heals -- a pattern called eventual consistency.

It is worth being precise about what CAP does and does not claim: it describes behavior specifically during a network partition, not a system's behavior in normal operation. Many real systems behave as CA-like most of the time and only reveal their true CAP trade-off when a partition actually occurs, which is part of why the theorem is frequently misapplied in casual system design discussions.
